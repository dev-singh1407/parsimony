What this is, before you watch it run. Your question does not go straight to the AI. It passes through six parts first, each with one job, and each able to leave the question exactly as it found it. Nothing here needs the internet: every part of this runs on the laptop in front of you.

Without this in the middle, the model reads every word you send, one at a time, before it writes anything back. That reading is most of the wait. Send it less and the wait gets shorter — as long as nothing that mattered was taken out, which is what the rest of this page is about.

The six parts in the order they run

M6

Shortcut router

Looks at the question first. If it is arithmetic, a date, or a simple lookup, it answers on the spot and the AI is never called.

For exampleAsk it 17 × 4 and it replies 68. Nothing is sent anywhere, and nothing is generated.

M2

Memory of past answers

Checks whether you have asked something close enough to this before. If so, the stored answer is reused instead of being written again.

For exampleAsk the same question twice and the second one comes back at once. Reword it slightly and it still matches, but only if the match is close enough to be safe.

M3

Conversation trimmer

In a long chat, most of what was said earlier has nothing to do with the new question. This keeps the turns that matter and drops the rest, then puts them in a fixed order.

For exampleTwenty turns about three different topics become the four turns that touch the one you just asked about.

M1

Document compressor

The big one. You attach a document; most of it is irrelevant to your question. Every sentence is scored, and only the ones that earn their place are sent. It then tidies spacing, drops repeated sentences, and shortens wordy phrasing.

For exampleA 49-sentence staff handbook goes in. Twelve sentences come out, and the answer is still right. That is 823 words down to 219.

M4

Prompt builder

Puts the parts that never change at the very front. The AI can reuse the work it already did on those instead of reading them again from scratch.

For exampleThe system instructions sit in the same place every time, so the model skips straight past them.

M5

Answer-length budget

Decides how long the answer is allowed to be before it starts. A question with a one-line answer does not need five hundred words.

For exampleA factual question is capped at 128 words. An open-ended one is given more room.

The two parts that are not steps

M8

The safety check

Nothing above is allowed to change your question by itself. Each part can only propose a change. M8 reads the proposal, compares what the text meant before and after, and puts it back if the meaning moved. It cannot be skipped, switched off, or implemented differently by one part than another.

For exampleTwo sentences give two different dates. The repeat-remover thinks the second is a duplicate and asks to delete it. M8 refuses, because the dates differ. The saving is lost on purpose.

M7

Learning from old chats

This one does not run while you wait. It runs separately, afterwards, over conversations that already happened, and writes down what it found so the system does not start from nothing the next day.

For exampleIf you explain the same background every morning, it notices, and that background is pinned where the model can reuse it.

Drag the relevance floor above and the chart moves with it. Everything below the line is dropped, so that dial decides how much survives — and the shipped 0.15 is a choice with a measured price, not a constant. On a public multi-hop benchmark, moving it to 0.05 recovers the answer on 10% more questions and costs 14 points of context (ADR-050). Moving it here changes this page only; the system keeps its own setting.

The token budget is the other constraint, and the pair is the finding: on that benchmark, raising the budget saturates at 85% of answers recovered while lowering the floor reaches 90%. Six mechanisms were built to find more sentences before this was swept; the sentences were being found and then discarded. Move both and watch which one is actually biting.
Why this is not a cosmetic switch. The encoder produces the scores every threshold on this page is read against, so it decides what survives. Thresholds calibrated for one do not transfer to the other (ADR-041): the lexical encoder never scored an adversarial cache pair above 0.85, so an accept zone looked safe; the neural one puts the same pairs straight through it. Two arms of a study once came out identical because the encoder resolved the same way for both, and the one-item gap between them was nothing but a per-prompt nonce (ADR-046).

Switch it and watch the bars move. Where the two disagree about which sentences matter is where every result in this project has to name which one produced it — including the floor tables, which ship as two.
Why a rule beats a number. ADR-050 measured that no single constant is right: 0.15 suits a question whose evidence is concentrated under one high score, and refuses the second hop of a multi-hop question, which scores low against the question because it matches what the first hop said. So the floor stops being a number and becomes a reading of the sorted scores — find where they fall off a cliff, measured as a ratio between adjacent ranks rather than a difference. A collapse from 0.22 to 0.03 is a difference of only 0.19; a flat band of strong sentences, 1.00 to 0.75, is a bigger one. Differences near the top of the range swamp collapses near the bottom, which is backwards. A halving is a halving wherever it lands.

No cliff means the scores are a smooth ramp, and the honest reading of that is not “be permissive” but “this says nothing” — so the constant is used. The rule only ever departs from measured behaviour where it has something to depart on.

Measured, model-free, on 81 held-out corpus items with evidence (parsimony floor). Under the encoder that ships, it keeps exactly the same evidence as the constant — 100 of 101 answer spans, both — while sending 26.6% of the context against 27.0%, and on questions the documents cannot answer, 4.2% against 5.3%: a fifth less, because nothing there produces a cliff worth cutting at. Lowering the constant to 0.05, which is what ADR-050 recommended, buys nothing here and costs 6.8 points.

It is off by default, and that is the honest part: 0.4 points of context is not a reason to move a default four ADRs of measurement sit behind. What it replaces is the remedy — reach for this instead of the lower constant (ADR-051).

The two runs happen one after the other, never at the same time. This laptop has one processor. If both ran at once they would slow each other down, and the test would be measuring that instead of anything useful. Each run also gets a hidden tag that makes it unique, so the second one cannot quietly reuse the first one's answer and look fast.
